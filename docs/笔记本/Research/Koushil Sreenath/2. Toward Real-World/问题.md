## 1. MARL — Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

MARL stands for **Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning**, meaning:

> multiple agents learn how to make decisions in the same environment.

In this paper, each quadruped robot is an **agent**.

For example, in a 2v1 soccer game:

- Attacker 1 is one agent
- Attacker 2 is one agent
- Defender 1 is another agent

Unlike single-agent RL, the difficulty in MARL is that:

$$
\text{the environment changes not only because of physics, but also because other agents are acting}
$$

So one robot's optimal action depends on:

- where the ball is
- where teammates are
- where opponents are
- what teammates may do next
- what opponents may do next

This paper studies a **mixed cooperative-competitive MARL problem**:

- robots on the same team cooperate
- robots on different teams compete

The goal is to learn team-level strategies such as:

- passing
- interception
- coordinated attacking
- coordinated defending
- dynamic role allocation

## 2. MAPPO — Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization

MAPPO stands for:

**Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization**

It is the reinforcement learning algorithm used in this paper to train the **high-level strategy policy**.

A simple way to understand it is:

> MAPPO is a multi-agent version of PPO.

The low-level skills such as:

- Walk
- Dribble
- Kick

are first trained separately.

Then the high-level policy decides things like:

> Which skill should I use now?

and

> In which direction should I execute this skill?

In this paper, MAPPO is used to train this high-level decision-making policy.

So the hierarchy is roughly:

$$
\text{High-level policy trained with MAPPO}
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
\text{choose Walk / Dribble / Kick + direction}
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
\text{low-level skill policy controls the robot joints}
$$

An important idea is that robots on the same team share the same high-level policy network.

## 3. FSP — Fictitious Self-Play(虚拟自博弈)

> train against multiple previous versions of the opponent instead of only the latest opponent.

Suppose the defender has gone through several versions:

$$
D_0,\ D_1,\ D_2,\ D_3
$$

When training a new attacker, the attacker does not only fight against $D_3$.

Instead, it may randomly fight against:

$$
\{D_0, D_1, D_2, D_3\}
$$

In this paper, attackers and defenders are trained **alternately**.

For example:

$$
A_0
\rightarrow
D_0
\rightarrow
A_1
\rightarrow
D_1
\rightarrow
A_2
\rightarrow
D_2
$$

When one side is being trained, the opponent population is frozen.

After the current policy becomes sufficiently strong, its snapshot is added to the policy population, and training switches to the other side.

### Why is FSP useful?

If the attacker always trains against only one fixed defender, it may learn a strategy that only works against that specific defender.

This can cause the policy to fall into a **local optimum**.

FSP exposes the agent to many different opponent strategies, so it learns more diverse behaviors.

For example, the attacker may learn to choose between:

- passing
- solo running
- shooting
- rebounding

depending on the defender's behavior.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{FSP = repeatedly training against a population of previous opponent policies}
}
$$

## 4. Dynamic Role Allocation

**Dynamic role allocation** means:

> robots do not have permanently fixed roles. Their roles change depending on the current game situation.

For example, in a 2v1 attack, the paper does not explicitly define:

- Attacker 1 must always dribble
- Attacker 2 must always receive the pass

Instead, the robots learn to determine their roles dynamically.

At one moment:

- Attacker 1 may control the ball
- Attacker 2 may move to a good receiving position

Later, the situation may change:

- Attacker 2 may become the main ball carrier
- Attacker 1 may support or prepare for a pass

So:

$$
\text{role at time } t
\neq
\text{fixed identity of the robot}
$$

## 5. Ablation Study

An **ablation study** is an experiment used to answer:

> Which component of the proposed method is actually useful?

Suppose a method contains:

$$
A+B+C
$$

and achieves good performance.

To test whether $C$ is useful, we can compare:

$$
A+B+C
$$

with:

$$
A+B
$$

If removing $C$ causes performance to become significantly worse, then $C$ is probably an important component.

This is called an **ablation study**.

# Decentralized Policy and Egocentric Observation

The paper states:

- **decentralized**

## 1. What does "decentralized" mean?

**Decentralized** means that each robot makes its **own decision independently**, instead of using one central controller to decide the actions of all robots.

### Centralized decision-making

In a centralized system, one central controller observes the situation and decides what every robot should do:

$$
\text{Central Controller}
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{Robot 1 action}\\
\text{Robot 2 action}\\
\text{Robot 3 action}
\end{cases}
$$

For example, the central controller may decide:

- Robot 1 should dribble
- Robot 2 should move to the right
- Robot 3 should defend

All decisions are made by one central "brain".

### Decentralized decision-making

In this paper, each robot makes its own decision.

For Robot 1:

$$
o_1
\rightarrow
\pi_{\text{high}}
\rightarrow
a_1
$$

For Robot 2:

$$
o_2
\rightarrow
\pi_{\text{high}}
\rightarrow
a_2
$$

For Robot 3:

$$
o_3
\rightarrow
\pi_{\text{high}}
\rightarrow
a_3
$$

Here:

- $o_i$ is the observation of robot $i$
- $\pi_{\text{high}}$ is the high-level policy
- $a_i$ is the action selected by robot $i$

Each robot receives its own observation and independently chooses its own action.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Decentralized}
=
\text{each robot makes its own decision}
}
$$

An important detail is that robots on the same team can still use the **same policy network**.

That does not make the system centralized.

For example:

$$
a_1 = \pi_{\text{high}}(o_1)
$$

$$
a_2 = \pi_{\text{high}}(o_2)
$$

The robots use the same policy parameters, but because they receive different observations, they can produce different actions.

So:

$$
\text{same policy network}
\neq
\text{centralized control}
$$

The key point is that there is no single controller making one joint decision for all robots.

## 2. What does "egocentric observation" mean?

**Egocentric** means:

> viewing or representing the environment from the robot's own perspective.

Each robot describes the world relative to itself.

For example, suppose the ball is at some fixed position on the field.

Robot 1 may observe:

```text
Ball:
2 m in front
1 m to the left
```

while Robot 2 may observe the same ball as:

```text
Ball:
1 m behind
3 m to the right
```

The ball has not changed its actual position.

The difference comes from the fact that:

$$
\text{Robot 1 viewpoint}
\neq
\text{Robot 2 viewpoint}
$$

So each robot has its own egocentric observation.

In this paper, the high-level policy receives information such as the relative positions of:

- the ball
- teammates
- opponents
- opponent goal
- its own goal

These positions are represented relative to the robot itself.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Egocentric observation}
=
\text{the world represented from each robot's own viewpoint}
}
$$
## 3. Why are decentralized and egocentric used together?

These two ideas naturally fit together.

Because each robot makes its own decision, each robot needs to make that decision based on the information available from its own perspective.

So the system becomes:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Robot's own observation}
\rightarrow
\text{Robot's own decision}
}
$$

For example:

$$
o_1
\rightarrow
\pi_{\text{high}}
\rightarrow
a_1
$$

$$
o_2
\rightarrow
\pi_{\text{high}}
\rightarrow
a_2
$$

Robot 1 and Robot 2 may observe the same soccer field differently, and therefore they may choose different actions.

## 4. What does "compose the low-level skills" mean?

The high-level policy does not directly control the robot's joints.

Instead, it chooses among low-level skills such as:

$$
\text{Walk},\quad
\text{Dribble},\quad
\text{Kick}
$$

and also selects a direction.

For example, Robot 1 may choose:

$$
\text{Dribble + Right}
$$

while Robot 2 chooses:

$$
\text{Walk + Forward-Right}
$$

The selected low-level skill then handles the detailed joint control.

So the overall structure is:

$$
\text{Egocentric Observation}
\rightarrow
\text{High-Level Policy}
\rightarrow
\text{Select Skill + Direction}
\rightarrow
\text{Low-Level Policy}
\rightarrow
\text{Joint Motion}
$$

## 5. Final Interpretation of the Sentence

The sentence:

> "we train a decentralized high-level policy that learns to compose the low-level skills based on egocentric observation"

can be rewritten as:

> Each robot independently uses a high-level policy to decide which low-level skill to execute, based on how that robot itself observes the current soccer situation.

The two most important distinctions are:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Decentralized}
=
\text{who makes the decision}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Egocentric}
=
\text{from whose viewpoint the observation is represented}
}
$$

### Analysis

## 1. What is CTDE?

**CTDE** stands for:

**Centralized Training, Decentralized Execution**

It is a common framework in **Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL)**.

The key idea is:

> During training, the learning algorithm can use information from multiple agents or even the whole environment.  
> During execution, each agent must make decisions independently based on its own local observation.

So:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Centralized Training}
+
\text{Decentralized Execution}
}
$$

## 2. Centralized Training

Suppose there are three robots:

$$
A_1,\quad A_2,\quad D_1
$$

During training, the learning algorithm may have access to the global state of the whole soccer game.

For example, it may know:

- the position of Robot 1
- the position of Robot 2
- the position of the defender
- the position of the ball
- the actions of multiple agents
- other simulator information

We can represent this global information as:

$$
s_t
=
\text{global state of the whole environment}
$$

Because the training algorithm can use information from all agents together, this is called:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Centralized Training}
}
$$

The main purpose is to make multi-agent learning easier and more stable.
## 3. Decentralized Execution

After training is finished, the robots are deployed in the real world.

At this stage, there is no central controller deciding the actions of all robots.

Each robot receives its own local observation and independently chooses its own action.

For Robot 1:

$$
o_1
\rightarrow
\pi_1
\rightarrow
a_1
$$

For Robot 2:

$$
o_2
\rightarrow
\pi_2
\rightarrow
a_2
$$

Each robot only needs its own observation during execution.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Decentralized Execution}
=
\text{each robot makes its own decision independently}
}
$$

## 1. Desired Joint Position

The paper states:

> Each skill is realized by a neural network policy $\pi_\theta(a\mid o,c)$ which outputs desired joint positions at 50 Hz. These target joint positions are subsequently tracked by proportional–derivative (PD) controllers operating at 200 Hz with gains $K_p=35$, $K_d=0.5$.

The paper explicitly tells us:

- the neural network outputs **desired joint positions**
- the policy runs at **50 Hz**
- a **PD controller** tracks these desired joint positions
- the PD controller runs at **200 Hz**
- the gains are

$$
K_p=35,\qquad K_d=0.5
$$

The paper does not write out the full PD equation here, so the equations below are the standard interpretation of a PD position controller.

## 1. What Does the Neural Network Output?

The low-level neural network policy is written as:

$$
\pi_\theta(a\mid o,c)
$$

where:

- $o$ = observation
- $c$ = command
- $a$ = action
- $\theta$ = neural network parameters

In this paper, the action $a$ is the desired joint position.

For one joint, we can write the desired position as:

$$
q_{\text{des}}
$$

where:

- $q_{\text{des}}$ = desired joint position
- $q$ = actual joint position

For example:

$$
q_{\text{des}}=1.0
$$

means:

> the neural network wants this joint to be at position $1.0$.

If the real joint is currently at:

$$
q=0.4
$$

then the joint has not reached the desired position yet.

For the whole quadruped robot, the policy outputs many desired joint positions:

$$
a_t=
[
q_{1,\text{des}},
q_{2,\text{des}},
\dots,
q_{12,\text{des}}
]
$$

## 2. Why Do We Need a PD Controller?

The neural network only says:

> "I want the joint to be here."

For example:

$$
q_{\text{des}}=1.0
$$

But the motor still needs to decide:

> how strongly should I push the joint toward $1.0$?

That is the job of the PD controller.

The control pipeline is:

$$
\text{Neural Network}
\rightarrow
q_{\text{des}}
\rightarrow
\text{PD Controller}
\rightarrow
\text{Motor Command}
\rightarrow
\text{Actual Joint Motion}
$$

So the neural network chooses the target.

The PD controller makes the real joint follow that target.

# 3. Position Error

The first important quantity is the position error:

$$
e
=
q_{\text{des}}-q
$$

where:

- $q_{\text{des}}$ = desired joint position
- $q$ = actual joint position
- $e$ = position error

For example:

$$
q_{\text{des}}=1.0
$$

and:

$$
q=0.4
$$

then:

$$
e
=
1.0-0.4
=
0.6
$$

This means the real joint is still $0.6$ away from the target.

# 4. P: Proportional

The **P** in PD means:

**Proportional**

"Proportional" means:

> the correction is proportional to the current position error.

The P term is:

$$
K_p e
$$

Since:

$$
e=q_{\text{des}}-q
$$

we can write:

$$
\boxed{
\text{P term}
=
K_p(q_{\text{des}}-q)
}
$$

The idea is:

> the farther the joint is from the target, the stronger the correction.


## Example

Suppose:

$$
q_{\text{des}}=1.0
$$

and:

$$
q=0.4
$$

Then:

$$
q_{\text{des}}-q
=
0.6
$$

The paper uses:

$$
K_p=35
$$

Therefore:

$$
K_p(q_{\text{des}}-q)
=
35\times0.6
=
21
$$

Now suppose the joint is much closer:

$$
q=0.9
$$

Then:

$$
q_{\text{des}}-q
=
0.1
$$

so:

$$
35\times0.1
=
3.5
$$

Therefore:

- large position error $\rightarrow$ large correction
- small position error $\rightarrow$ small correction

So:

$$
\boxed{
P
=
\text{react to position error}
}
$$

# 5. What Is $K_p$?

$K_p$ is called the **proportional gain**.

You can think of it as:

> how strongly the controller reacts to position error.

So:

$$
\boxed{
K_p
=
\text{position correction strength}
}
$$


# 6. D: Derivative

The **D** in PD means:

**Derivative**

Here, derivative means:

> how fast something is changing.

The important point is:

**The D term looks at how fast the position error is changing.**

We already defined the position error as:

$$
e
=
q_{\text{des}}-q
$$

Now take the derivative with respect to time:

$$
\dot e
=
\frac{d}{dt}(q_{\text{des}}-q)
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\dot e
=
\dot q_{\text{des}}
-
\dot q
}
$$

where:

- $\dot q_{\text{des}}$ = desired joint velocity
- $\dot q$ = actual joint velocity

So the D term is:

$$
K_d\dot e
$$

or equivalently:

$$
\boxed{
\text{D term}
=
K_d
(
\dot q_{\text{des}}
-
\dot q
)
}
$$

This is the **velocity difference**.

# 7. Why Does Velocity Appear in the D Term?

Because:

$$
q
$$

is position.

Therefore:

$$
\dot q
=
\frac{dq}{dt}
$$

is velocity.

Similarly:

$$
q_{\text{des}}
$$

is desired position.

Therefore:

$$
\dot q_{\text{des}}
=
\frac{dq_{\text{des}}}{dt}
$$

is desired velocity.

So:

$$
q_{\text{des}}-q
$$

is the position difference.

But:

$$
\dot q_{\text{des}}-\dot q
$$

is the velocity difference.

This distinction is very important:

$$
\boxed{
P:
q_{\text{des}}-q
=
\text{position error}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
D:
\dot q_{\text{des}}-\dot q
=
\text{velocity error}
}
$$
# 8. Why Is the D Term Useful?

Suppose the target joint position is:

$$
q_{\text{des}}=1.0
$$

and the target is currently fixed.

If the target is fixed, then:

$$
\dot q_{\text{des}}=0
$$

Now suppose the actual joint is already close:

$$
q=0.95
$$

So the position error is:

$$
q_{\text{des}}-q
=
1.0-0.95
=
0.05
$$

The P term sees:

> the joint is already very close to the target.

But now suppose the actual joint is still moving very quickly toward the target:

$$
\dot q=2
$$

Since:

$$
\dot q_{\text{des}}=0
$$

the velocity error is:

$$
\dot q_{\text{des}}-\dot q
=
0-2
=
-2
$$

Therefore the D term produces a negative correction.

Intuitively, it says:

> you are already close to the target, but you are moving too fast, so slow down.

This is why the D term behaves like damping or braking.

So:

$$
\boxed{
D
=
\text{react to how fast the position error is changing}
}
$$

and in the common case where the desired position is temporarily fixed:

$$
\boxed{
D
\approx
\text{braking based on actual joint velocity}
}
$$

# 9. What Is $K_d$?

$K_d$ is called the **derivative gain**.

It controls how strongly the controller reacts to the velocity error:

$$
\dot q_{\text{des}}-\dot q
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{
K_d
=
\text{damping / braking strength}
}
$$

A larger $K_d$ means:

- stronger damping
- stronger resistance to fast motion
- less overshooting and oscillation

A smaller $K_d$ means:

- weaker damping
- less braking effect

### Analysis
# 3.3 High-Level Multi-Agent Strategy Policy

## 1. What is Section 3.3 trying to do?

Section 3.2 has already trained several low-level skills:

$$
\text{Walk},\quad \text{Dribble},\quad \text{Kick}
$$

Section 3.3 is no longer learning **how to move the joints**.

Instead, it trains a high-level policy to decide:

1. **which skill to use**
2. **which direction to execute that skill**

So the main idea is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{High-level policy}
=
\text{choose skill}
+
\text{choose direction}
}
$$

For example:

$$
\text{Dribble + Right}
$$

or:

$$
\text{Kick + Up-Right}
$$

The selected command is then given to the corresponding low-level policy.

---

# 2. Question 1: Why are the directions discretized into only 8 directions?

Originally, the low-level command is:

$$
c=(v_x,v_y)
$$

which is continuous.

That means the direction could theoretically be any angle, such as:

$$
30^\circ,\quad 37^\circ,\quad 83.5^\circ
$$

However, the high-level policy does not directly output an arbitrary continuous direction.

The paper discretizes the direction into 8 equally spaced directions:

$$
0^\circ,\ 45^\circ,\ 90^\circ,\dots
$$

So the high-level policy chooses one of only eight directions.

This means that it cannot directly express an exact direction such as:

$$
30^\circ
$$

This is a deliberate simplification.

The advantage is that the high-level action space becomes much smaller and easier for reinforcement learning to explore.

So there is a trade-off:

$$
\boxed{
\text{less precise control}
\leftrightarrow
\text{easier and more efficient training}
}
$$

The paper explicitly says that the discretization is introduced to improve training efficiency.

---

# 3. Question 2: What does it mean that the magnitude of $c$ is pre-specified?

A 2D velocity command can be separated into:

$$
\text{direction}
+
\text{magnitude}
$$

For example:

$$
c=(v_x,v_y)
$$

has magnitude:

$$
\|c\|
$$

and a direction.

In a more flexible system, the high-level policy could decide both:

$$
\text{direction}
$$

and:

$$
\text{speed}
$$

For example:

$$
(\text{Walk},30^\circ,0.5\text{ m/s})
$$

or:

$$
(\text{Walk},30^\circ,1.5\text{ m/s})
$$

However, this paper simplifies the problem.

The high-level policy mainly decides:

$$
\boxed{
\text{skill type + direction}
}
$$

while the magnitude is already specified for each skill.

For example, for Walk:

$$
\|c\|=v_{\text{walk}}
$$

So if the policy chooses:

$$
\text{Walk + Right}
$$

the resulting command is roughly:

$$
c=(v_{\text{walk}},0)
$$

rather than allowing the high-level policy to freely choose any walking speed.

Again, this sacrifices some flexibility in exchange for a smaller and easier action space.

So:

$$
\boxed{
\text{High-level policy does not have full continuous control over }c
}
$$

---

# 4. Question 3: What information does the high-level policy receive?

The high-level policy receives information such as:

- the robot's own state
- relative position of the ball
- relative positions of teammates
- relative positions of opponents
- relative positions of both goals

The relative positions are **egocentric**.

This means they are represented from the current robot's own viewpoint.

For example:

$$
p_{\text{ball}}=(2,-1)
$$

could mean:

> the ball is 2 meters in front of me and 1 meter to my right.

So the high-level policy does not simply receive one global "god-view" description of the whole field.

---

# 5. Question 4: Why does the high-level policy use a GRU?

The paper says the high-level policy uses a GRU to capture **long-term dependencies**.

The reason is that the current decision may depend on what happened in previous moments.

For example, one single frame may show that an opponent is on the left.

But one frame alone may not tell us whether the opponent is:

- moving left
- moving right
- approaching us
- moving away from us

Therefore, the high-level policy needs some memory of previous observations.

The GRU maintains a hidden state:

$$
h_t
$$

which can be understood as a compressed memory of previous information.

So the decision is approximately based on:

$$
\text{current observation}
+
\text{past memory}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{GRU}
\approx
\text{a neural network with memory}
}
$$

# 6. Question 5: Why does the high-level policy make one decision every 10 low-level steps?

The low-level policy operates at:

$$
50\text{ Hz}
$$

so it produces a new joint action every:

$$
0.02\text{ s}
$$

The high-level policy operates at:

$$
5\text{ Hz}
$$

so it makes a new high-level decision every:

$$
0.2\text{ s}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
1\text{ high-level decision}
=
10\text{ low-level steps}
}
$$

This does **not** mean that the low-level policy acts independently of the high-level command.

Instead, the high-level command is held for 10 low-level steps.

For example, at:

$$
t=0
$$

the high-level policy may choose:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Dribble + Right}
}
$$

For the next 0.2 seconds, the Dribble policy keeps receiving the same general command:

$$
c=\text{Right}
$$

but it receives a new low-level observation at every timestep.

So:

$$
(o_0,c)
\rightarrow
a_0
$$

$$
(o_1,c)
\rightarrow
a_1
$$

$$
(o_2,c)
\rightarrow
a_2
$$

$$
\vdots
$$

$$
(o_9,c)
\rightarrow
a_9
$$

The command $c$ is the same, but:

$$
o_0\neq o_1\neq o_2
$$

because the robot is continuously moving.

Therefore the low-level joint actions can also be different:

$$
a_0\neq a_1\neq a_2
$$

The important distinction is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{High-level}
=
\text{what should I do?}
}
$$

For example:

$$
\text{Dribble + Right}
$$

while:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Low-level}
=
\text{how should my joints move right now?}
}
$$

So their frequencies do not need to be equal.

It decides:

# 8. Question 6: What are the two Softmax heads?

The high-level neural network has two separate output heads.

The first head determines the probability distribution over skills.

For example:

$$
P(\text{Walk})=0.1
$$

$$
P(\text{Dribble})=0.6
$$

$$
P(\text{Kick})=0.2
$$

$$
P(\text{Stop})=0.1
$$

The second head determines the probability distribution over the eight directions.

For example:

$$
P(\text{Right})=0.6
$$

$$
P(\text{Up-Right})=0.2
$$

and smaller probabilities for the remaining directions.

So the two heads separately decide:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Head 1: skill}
}
$$

and:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Head 2: direction}
}
$$

The final high-level action may therefore be:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Dribble + Right}
}
$$

Softmax simply converts the network's outputs into probabilities that sum to 1.

# 9. Question 7: If execution is decentralized, why do agents share one policy network?

**Decentralized** does not mean that every robot must have a completely different neural network.

It means that every robot independently computes its own action using its own observation.

For two robots:

$$
a_1=\pi(o_1)
$$

$$
a_2=\pi(o_2)
$$

They may use the same network parameters:

$$
\pi
$$

but their observations are different:

$$
o_1\neq o_2
$$

so their actions can also be different:

$$
a_1\neq a_2
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{same policy parameters}
\neq
\text{centralized control}
}
$$

The important requirement for decentralized execution is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{each robot uses its own observation to compute its own action}
}
$$

# 10. Question 8: What does "all agents on the same team share a common high-level policy network" mean?

A convenient way to understand the paper is to treat:

- Attacker Team
- Defender Team

as two different teams.

Within the Attacker Team:

$$
A_1,A_2,\dots
\rightarrow
\pi_{\text{att}}
$$

Within the Defender Team:

$$
D_1,D_2,\dots
\rightarrow
\pi_{\text{def}}
$$

So robots on the same team share the same high-level policy parameters.

However, they still receive different observations and therefore can produce different actions.

Also, "Attacker" and "Defender" should not be interpreted as temporary roles that switch every time possession changes.

They are better understood as the identities of the two competing teams in the training setup.

A Defender can still:

- intercept the ball
- dribble
- counterattack
- score

without switching to the Attacker policy.

Likewise, the Attacker policy can react after losing possession without suddenly switching to the Defender policy.

So:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Attacker/Defender}
=
\text{team identity in the training setup}
}
$$

rather than:

$$
\text{temporary offensive/defensive role at every moment}
$$

Section 3.4 then trains attacker and defender policies against each other through FSP.

# 11. Question 9: What does "dense shaping" mean?

The phrase is really shorthand for:

$$
\boxed{
\text{dense reward shaping}
}
$$

The word **shaping** comes from **reward shaping**.

Reward shaping means:

> adding extra rewards to guide the agent toward useful behavior.

The word **dense** means that these rewards occur relatively frequently.

This contrasts with sparse rewards.

For example:

$$
\text{score a goal}
\rightarrow
+\text{large reward}
$$

may happen only rarely.

That is a sparse reward.

But the agent may receive smaller intermediate rewards for things such as:

- moving the ball forward
- avoiding collisions
- staying inside the field

These can happen much more frequently.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{dense reward shaping}
=
\text{frequent intermediate rewards used to guide learning}
}
$$

# 12. Sparse Rewards + Dense Reward Shaping

The high-level reward combines two types of reward.

## Sparse outcome rewards

For example:

$$
\text{score}
\rightarrow
+\text{reward}
$$

$$
\text{concede}
\rightarrow
-\text{reward}
$$

These directly represent the final objective of soccer.

---

## Dense shaping rewards

These encourage useful intermediate behavior, such as:

- reducing collisions
- avoiding leaving the field
- moving the ball toward the opponent's goal

So the reward structure can be understood as:

$$
\boxed{
R
=
R_{\text{sparse}}
+
R_{\text{dense shaping}}
}
$$

# 13. Question 10: Why is cooperation called "implicit"?

The paper does not explicitly reward behaviors such as:

$$
\text{passing}
\rightarrow
+\text{reward}
$$

or:

$$
\text{cooperation}
\rightarrow
+\text{reward}
$$

Instead, the robots are rewarded for accomplishing the soccer objective.

For example:

$$
\text{score}
\rightarrow
+\text{reward}
$$

If passing makes scoring more likely, then the policy may eventually learn to pass.

So:

$$
\text{passing}
\rightarrow
\text{higher probability of scoring}
\rightarrow
\text{higher expected reward}
$$

The passing behavior therefore emerges naturally.

This is called:

$$
\boxed{
\text{implicit cooperation}
}
$$

because the researchers did not explicitly program:

> "You must cooperate."

The cooperation emerges because cooperation helps accomplish the task.

# 14. Full Summary of Section 3.3

The high-level policy observes the current soccer situation from the robot's own perspective.

It also uses a GRU to retain information from previous observations.

So its input is roughly:

$$
\boxed{
\text{current local observation}
+
\text{GRU memory}
}
$$

The high-level policy then produces two probability distributions:

$$
\boxed{
\text{skill distribution}
}
$$

and:

$$
\boxed{
\text{direction distribution}
}
$$

It selects:

$$
\text{Walk / Dribble / Kick / Stop}
$$

and one of eight directions.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
a_{\text{high}}
=
(\text{skill},\text{direction})
}
$$

The magnitude of the command is pre-specified for each skill rather than freely chosen by the high-level policy.

The high-level decision is updated at:

$$
5\text{ Hz}
$$

so one high-level action is used for:

$$
10
$$

low-level control steps.

During those 10 steps, the low-level policy continuously observes the changing robot state and generates new joint targets at:

$$
50\text{ Hz}
$$

The reward contains:

$$
\text{sparse task outcomes}
+
\text{dense reward shaping}
$$

but does not explicitly reward cooperation.

Behaviors such as passing and coordination arise because they help the robots achieve the final task objective.
